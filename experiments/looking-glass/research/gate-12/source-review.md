# Gate 12 source review: coordinate without erasing disagreement

Accessed 2026-09-20. Collector: GPT-5.6 Luna via `web__run`. This bounded review gathers three primary sources for the approved Gate 12 instrument: two explicit local references, retained incompatible judgments, and an inspectable joint action. In the current fixture, all source facts in the public export mode are common; the local reference functions apply different objectives (detail versus speed) to those same facts. The sources motivate preservation and coordination checks; they do not show that this software creates human coordination, agreement, trust, or learning. Agreement is one possible outcome, not the sole success criterion.

## Primary methodological findings

**[Star and Griesemer (1989), “Institutional Ecology, ‘Translations’ and Boundary Objects,” Social Studies of Science](https://doi.org/10.1177/030631289019003001)** ([original-paper PDF copy](https://griesemer.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/07-star-griesemer-1989-sss19-3387-420-boundary-objects.pdf)). Their museum case develops “boundary objects” as artifacts adaptable to several local viewpoints while robust enough to retain a common identity; they also describe standardization and translation as ways heterogeneous actors cooperate despite divergent viewpoints. For Gate 12, the shared decision record can be a stable joint object while each local reference, judgment, and rationale remains separately addressable. This supports preserving disagreement and checking that the joint action points back to both local inputs. It does not establish that a displayed object is understood similarly by human users or that disagreement has been resolved.

**[Schmidt and Bannon (1992), “Taking CSCW Seriously: Supporting Articulation Work,” Computer Supported Cooperative Work](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00752449)** ([author-uploaded full text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/226480952_Taking_CSCW_seriously_Supporting_Articulation_Work)). This programmatic primary paper treats articulation work as assembling, scheduling, monitoring, and coordinating steps under local contingencies and incommensurate opinions. Its common-information-space discussion distinguishes shared artifacts from shared meaning: a common database alone is insufficient, and meanings are negotiated locally and temporarily by semi-autonomous actors. Gate 12 should therefore record each local reference and judgment before any joint action, retain the action’s scope and unresolved conflicts, and avoid replacing two inputs with a single consensus field. This is a conceptual CSCW framework, not a result that the instrument improves real cooperative work.

**[Dahleh, Tahbaz-Salehi, Tsitsiklis, and Zoumpoulis (2015 online / 2016 issue), “Coordination with Local Information,” Operations Research](https://doi.org/10.1287/opre.2015.1378)** ([corrected institutional PDF](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/11553/Coordination_Tahbaz-Salehi.pdf)). In a finite-player global-games model, local signals observed by only subsets of agents can produce multiple equilibria; the pattern of information sharing affects whether coordination is determinate. This is adjacent formal context only: unlike the Gate 12 fixture, it studies local information sets and strategic equilibria. It is neither the implemented model nor evidence for the fixture’s objective disagreement. The software boundary condition is narrower: record all public facts as common, represent each local reference as an explicit function/objective over those facts, and do not infer a single shared judgment merely because a joint action exists.

## Gate 12 constraints

1. Freeze the common public export facts, then freeze at least two local reference functions/objectives and their judgments before constructing the joint action. Preserve each source, timestamp/revision, function/objective, judgment, and incompatibility as first-class records.
2. Make the joint action inspectable: it must list participating local judgments, its decision scope, and any unresolved disagreement. A joint action may coordinate a next step without making the local judgments identical.
3. Distinguish common public facts from local reference functions/objectives. Any merge, omission, or tie-break rule must be declared in the finite menu and replayable; an action must not silently overwrite or average incompatible values or imply hidden local information.
4. Validate the diagram/semantic view against an equivalent plain representation with the same local references, disagreement records, action fields, and provenance. Software checks establish preservation and traceability for the frozen fixtures only.

## Source limits

Star and Griesemer’s case and Schmidt and Bannon’s CSCW framework are conceptual and historically situated; Dahleh et al. is a formal coordination model with strategic assumptions. None establishes that people will coordinate, that consensus is desirable, or that this instrument improves judgment. Any human coordination claim would require actual participants, separate recorded local judgments, and observed joint outcomes under a separately approved protocol.
