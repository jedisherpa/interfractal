## Torus, cones, and responsible change — working model v0.2

Developed for Paul Cooper / Jedi Sherpa · 13 September 2026

**Core idea:** Keep room to see differently, make commitments that others can rely on, and revise without erasing people or consequences.

This model has three deliberately distinct parts: an exact mathematical construction, a practical coordination protocol, and a contemplative/child-friendly interpretation. The construction is not a proof that consciousness, human relationships, or the Metacanon Constitution literally has Hopf topology.

The useful result is stronger than a resemblance between drawings: there is an explicit construction connecting circles, cones, tori, and the Hopf fibration. There is also an executable protocol that preserves the distinctions developed in the earlier possibility/commitment discussion. The bridge between those two parts is a proposed design interpretation, not an established psychological law.

## 1. The whole idea for a curious eight-year-old

Imagine a workshop with a doughnut-shaped table. On the table are things people are using: a dragon they are building, a plan for a parade, and promises about who will make the wings.

Above the table is a wide funnel full of things they might try. Below it is another funnel where they take apart ideas that need changing and save the useful pieces.

Maya says, “What if our dragon went over the bridge?” That puts an idea in the top funnel. It does not put Bea's name on a promise.

Bea can look at the idea, ask questions, suggest better wings, and still say, “I don't want to carry it over the bridge.” Being curious does not take away her no.

The friends test a small dragon. It falls over. They look carefully and find one short foot. They add one block. They do not have to throw away the entire dragon—or decide Finn is a bad builder.

Later, they agree on a checked route through the orchard. Now their plan belongs on the table, because everyone knows what they actually agreed to do.

If somebody needs to stop, the others cannot force them to continue. But they do need to talk about the unfinished work. And they write down what happened, so next time they can begin a little wiser.

Here is the surprising mathematics: sometimes you can move all the way around a loop and return to the same place, while something about your orientation has changed. It is like coming back to the same question after an adventure. We will use that as an image for learning—but our notebook, not a magic number, remembers the actual adventure.

**Dream freely. Ask clearly. Build carefully. Keep learning.**

## 2. The same idea in contemplative language

An experience can be allowed without being obeyed. A thought can be heard without becoming a command. A boundary can remain while an interpretation softens.

The upper cone is the willingness to meet what has not yet taken form. The lower cone is the willingness to release a form that no longer fits. The torus is the work of inhabiting and caring for what we have chosen.

None is a higher spiritual state. Sometimes the practice is opening; sometimes it is keeping a promise; sometimes it is resting; sometimes it is leaving.

Nonattachment, in this model, means relinquishing the demand that inquiry preserve my preferred conclusion. It does not mean relinquishing discernment, consent, or responsibility. Calmness is not a requirement for a valid objection. Someone else's distress does not make their no less real.

Returning to the same conclusion after sincere inquiry can still be meaningful. A changed conclusion is not the only evidence of learning. Nor should a person be required to undergo transformation to earn respect.

**Hold the form firmly enough to care for it, and lightly enough to learn from it.**

These are proposed contemplative practices, not claims about every religious tradition, enlightenment, or experimentally established mental geometry.

## 5. Exact mathematical construction

### 5.1 One whole circle can share one projection

Use two complex numbers with a unit-norm constraint:

\[
z=(z_1,z_2)\in S^3\subset\mathbb C^2,
\qquad |z_1|^2+|z_2|^2=1.
\]

The Hopf map is

\[
\pi(z)=\left(2\operatorname{Re}(z_1\overline z_2),
2\operatorname{Im}(z_1\overline z_2),|z_1|^2-|z_2|^2\right).
\]

An immediate calculation gives

\[
\|\pi(z)\|^2=4|z_1|^2|z_2|^2+(|z_1|^2-|z_2|^2)^2=1.
\]

Also \(\pi(e^{i\gamma}z)=\pi(z)\): the common phase cancels. Conversely, the displayed coordinates determine the rank-one matrix \(zz^\dagger\); equal projections give the same one-dimensional complex span and hence differ only by a unit complex scalar. Thus the fibres are exactly the common-phase circles.

This is standard Hopf geometry. An accessible primary mathematical exposition is David W. Lyons's [An Elementary Introduction to the Hopf Fibration](https://nilesjohnson.net/hopf-articles/Lyons_Elem-intro-Hopf-fibration.pdf). The cancellation and norm calculations above are included to make the implementation independently inspectable.

**Proposed interpretation:** distinguish a public description from the representations that leave it unchanged. Do not identify every difference between people with a gauge change; many perspectives change evidence, preference, or action and are therefore not redundant.

### 5.2 The torus appears without being added by hand

Choose coordinates

\[
z_1=\cos(\theta/2)e^{i\alpha},\qquad
z_2=\sin(\theta/2)e^{i\beta}.
\]

Substitution gives

\[
\pi(z)=(\sin\theta\cos(\alpha-\beta),
\sin\theta\sin(\alpha-\beta),\cos\theta).
\]

For each fixed \(0<\theta<\pi\), both angles vary independently: the level set is \(S^1\times S^1=T^2\). Fix their difference as well and only their common rotation remains: one Hopf fibre. At \(\theta=0\) or \(\pi\), one complex amplitude vanishes; the level set is a circle, not a torus.

The preimage of the base equator is the Clifford torus \(|z_1|=|z_2|=1/\sqrt2\). The torus represents a two-angle family of states; it is not the whole Hopf total space. Assigning it the human meaning “currently maintained forms” is our chosen interface, not an intrinsic meaning of the surface.

### 5.3 The strongest exact connection to your cones

The topological cone on a circle is a disk:

\[
C(S^1)=\big(S^1\times[0,1]\big)/\big(S^1\times\{0\}\big)\cong D^2.
\]

Join two such disks along their circular rims and obtain a sphere. It can be drawn as **two cone surfaces joined base-to-base**, with the shared rim as equator. This is not the apex-to-apex hourglass image.

Now retain a whole circle above every point of that sphere. Over either hemisphere, the Hopf bundle is a solid torus \(D^2\times S^1\). Its common boundary with the other solid torus is \(S^1\times S^1=T^2\).

A direct verification uses

\[
V_N=\{|z_1|\ge |z_2|\},\quad
V_S=\{|z_2|\ge |z_1|\}.
\]

On \(V_N\), write \(w=z_2/z_1\), \(|w|\le1\), and \(u=z_1/|z_1|\). The inverse parameterization is

\[
(w,u)\longmapsto u(1,w)/\sqrt{1+|w|^2}.
\]

This explicitly identifies \(V_N\) with \(D^2\times S^1\); exchange coordinates for \(V_S\). Their intersection is the equatorial torus. Their union is all of \(S^3\).

**In words: two cones, each carrying a circle of freedom, joined across a torus with the Hopf twist.**

The essential qualifier is *carrying a circle*. Cones alone do not make a Hopf fibration. If the fibre circle is collapsed at a cone tip, the resulting object is no longer this circle bundle. The apparent geometric tip in the base still has an entire circle above it.

The underlying cone, join, and Hopf constructions are covered in Allen Hatcher's [Algebraic Topology, Chapter 0 and Example 4.45](https://pi.math.cornell.edu/~hatcher/AT/AT.pdf). The explicit parameterization supplies a self-contained verification of the version used here.

### 5.4 The twist is a rule, not a visual flourish

Take local representatives

\[
s_N(\theta,\phi)=(\cos(\theta/2),\sin(\theta/2)e^{-i\phi}),
\]
\[
s_S(\theta,\phi)=(\cos(\theta/2)e^{i\phi},\sin(\theta/2)).
\]

They give the same base point, and on their overlap

\[
s_S=e^{i\phi}s_N.
\]

The transition winds once around the phase circle as \(\phi\) goes around the equator. That is the explicit ingredient the earlier governance proof *assumed* rather than derived from its governance data.

Keep two drawings separate: the original torus-and-funnels diagram is a process map; the two-hemisphere construction is a topology map. They can inform one another without pretending they are the same three-dimensional object.

## 6. A return that is not a reset

Use the north-chart lift

\[
z=e^{i\gamma}s_N(\theta,\phi),\qquad
A=\operatorname{Im}(z^\dagger dz)
=d\gamma-\sin^2(\theta/2)d\phi.
\]

Choose horizontal transport by requiring \(A(\dot z)=0\). Along a fixed latitude this requires

\[
\dot\gamma=\sin^2(\theta/2)\dot\phi.
\]

After one turn,

\[
\Delta\gamma=\pi(1-\cos\theta)\pmod{2\pi}.
\]

For \(\theta=60^\circ\), the base returns to its starting point while the lift has acquired a relative phase of \(90^\circ\). This is holonomy for the specified connection, not a property of an arbitrary walk with no transport rule.

The curvature descends to \(F=-\tfrac12\sin\theta\,d\theta\wedge d\phi\). Its integral divided by \(2\pi\) is \(-1\) with this real-connection and orientation convention; reversing appropriate conventions yields the opposite sign. The convention-independent statement needed here is unit absolute Chern number, not a positive moral meaning assigned to the sign.

Geometric phase has established physical uses; for context see Cisowski, Götte and Franke-Arnold's [Geometric phases of light: insights from fibre bundle theory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.04356). The calculation above is explicitly specified for our toy model. Its psychological interpretation remains an analogy.

The contemplative sentence is: **You can return to the same answer without pretending the journey never happened.** The practical implementation is an event history. Phase alone is not enough: different journeys can produce the same phase, and adding a full turn can make it disappear modulo \(2\pi\).

## 7. The practical model: geometry, permission, and memory

Represent a workshop state as

\[
X=(z,P,B,C,A,E,K,M).
\]

Here \(z\) is the geometric exploratory representation; \(P\) the versioned proposal; \(B\) the stated confidence; \(C\) explicit participant consent; \(A\) required authority; \(E\) the recorded bounded test; \(K\) safety/feasibility conditions; and \(M\) the event history and consequences.

This **augmented state space is not S³**. The S³ component is one constrained representation within it. In particular, a complete ledger of arbitrary histories has not been compressed into one quaternion.

The three modes now have executable meanings:

| Mode | Allowed operation | What does not happen automatically |
|---|---|---|
| Upper cone: generate | Add a candidate, explore alternatives, propose a new version. | No consent is inferred; no active commitment is silently cancelled. |
| Torus: maintain | Ratify a versioned agreement, act within it, monitor and care for it. | Confidence is not treated as permission; review remains possible. |
| Lower cone: release | Stop further participation, investigate an error, agree a response to consequences, retire a form. | History is not erased; a person's identity is not destroyed; nobody is compelled to continue. |

These modes are operations, not fixed personality types or global states of a person. One can maintain a promise while exploring another idea.

For the bounded *group-parade example*, a commitment gate requires the current proposal's consent, test, authority, and safety checks, plus resolution of conflicting existing commitments. This is not a universal rule that every personal choice requires everybody's consent; the relevant people and authority depend on the action.

The executable invariants are:

1. Common-phase changes do not create permission.
2. Confidence can change without changing commitment.
3. Consent is attached to an exact proposal version, not “whatever this idea becomes.”
4. A proposed replacement does not erase an existing agreement.
5. Withdrawing stops the simulated action immediately; addressing consequences is a separate process.
6. New events append to the history rather than rewriting prior events.
7. A failed test invites a scoped repair, not automatic rejection of everything.

The code simulates trusted inputs. It does not authenticate participants or infer consent, and it is not a deployed security boundary or a real bridge-safety system.

### A nontrivial, testable bridge to the geometry

The code embeds three sample ideas at three chosen directions on the base sphere and selects the largest dot-product score. The placement is arbitrary design data, not learned evidence about the plans. Because selection depends on \(\pi(z)\), common-phase changes leave it unchanged. Moving on the base can change it. Selecting an idea merely produces a suggestion; the permission gate remains separate.

This deliberately distinguishes **representational invariance**, **decision selection**, and **authorization**.

## 8. A modest theorem that the model can actually earn

Let \(D\) be a set of outputs and let \(a,m\) stand for authorization and history. A function

\[
f:S^3\times A\times M\to D
\]

is invariant under common-phase changes if and only if a unique function

\[
\bar f:S^2\times A\times M\to D
\]

satisfies

\[
f(z,a,m)=\bar f(\pi(z),a,m).
\]

**Proof.** If the factorization exists, the invariance follows from \(\pi(e^{i\gamma}z)=\pi(z)\). Conversely, all points with a given Hopf projection form exactly one common-phase orbit. Invariance therefore lets us define \(\bar f(n,a,m)\) using any representative above \(n\); the value is well-defined. Surjectivity supplies existence everywhere and uniqueness. This is a set-level theorem; suitable quotient-topology assumptions give the corresponding continuity statement.

This is a precise version of the useful part of your gauge-equivariance verification direction: an irrelevant representation change should not alter an output, while an actual authorization change may and often must.

An important limit follows. If \(D\) is discrete and \(f\) is continuous in \(z\), then at fixed \(a,m\) the output must be constant because \(S^3\) is connected. A useful finite choice policy therefore has decision boundaries, or returns continuous scores before a discrete selection step. The demo uses a deterministic tie rule. It does **not** pretend that finite decisions form a smooth S².

A no-global-section theorem is likewise not a no-tyranny theorem. The topological obstruction concerns a **continuous** choice of representative over every base point. An arbitrary discontinuous selector or an authoritarian rule is not ruled out by that obstruction.

## 10. The conjecture worth testing next

I would replace “the Constitution is literally the Hopf fibration” with two separately testable research claims:

**Structural claim:** A specifically defined class of coordination systems admits a useful quotient representation that separates public proposals from decision-preserving internal variation, while preserving explicit authority, stopping conditions, and historical consequences.

**Human-use claim:** Teaching that separation through the torus/cones/Hopf imagery improves understanding or coordination compared with the same operational rules taught without the imagery.

A literal Hopf correspondence is a stronger optional target, not assumed by either claim. To pursue it, define the actual total and base spaces, their topologies, the equivalence relation, a free circle action, local trivializations, and measured transition maps of winding ±1. Then demonstrate compatibility with the actual implementation rather than relabeling standard Hopf coordinates. Finite data require a justified geometric realization and error model; sampling a diagram is not enough.

Start with the same low-stakes task under three conditions: ordinary discussion; plain-language proposal/consent/commitment/review rules; and those same rules plus this geometry. Measure mistaken commitments, boundary-respecting refusals, completed or responsibly renegotiated actions, specific repairs, recall of lessons, and time cost. Do not score participants by how “open” they appear.

If the geometry adds confusion or makes refusal harder, change it or remove it. If the simpler rules work just as well, their value survives without a literal topological explanation. If the proposed state-space mapping fails, that counts against that mapping rather than against people's willingness to surrender.

## What this is / isn’t

This workshop uses exact Hopf geometry as a language for three separable moves: explore an idea, keep a versioned commitment, and revise without erasing people or history.

It is **not** a claim that the universe is a torus, that spacetime has “pixels,” or that topology proves physics, ethics, or consent. Returning to the same answer after a loop does not mean the journey never happened — and a beautiful shape never signs for you.

Keep two drawings separate: the hourglass is a *process* map (generate / maintain / release). The Hopf construction is a *topology* map. They can inform each other; they are not the same object.

## Closing statement

**A possibility deserves room to be considered. A commitment deserves care. A person retains the right to refuse. A revision carries its history forward.**

The torus helps us picture maintained form. The cones help us picture formation and release. Hopf geometry gives an exact example of a whole circle of variation sharing a projection, a torus emerging from two periodic freedoms, and a loop whose return need not be a reset.

Those are enough to build a serious model. They do not need to be inflated into a proof that a beautiful shape governs all of life.
